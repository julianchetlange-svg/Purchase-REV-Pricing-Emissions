Where the ROI Framework Gets It Wrong
Five specific challenges to the ROI-based decision framework, grounded in fleet operational reality
1
The ROI Framework Treats Replacement as Optional
Fundamental framing error — compares pre-buy to capital alternatives rather than cost of the same purchase later
Challenge
The Framework Says
Compare savings to hurdle rate and alternative uses of capital. If the return doesn't exceed ~10%, don't proceed. Treat it like an investment decision.
The Counter-Argument
These 43 assets must be replaced — 24 are already at or past lifecycle thresholds. The decision is not whether to buy, it's when and at what price. The correct comparison is: buy now at negotiated pricing vs. buy in 2027/2028 at standard pricing + emission upcharge. The cost of deferral is $1,837,000 in higher purchase prices alone — before maintenance costs on aging assets are included.
2
Ongoing Maintenance Costs Are Invisible in the Model
Deferral isn't cost-neutral — aging assets accumulate maintenance spend every month they stay in service
Challenge
The Framework Says
The savings of $574K (2027) and $434K (2028) are the only financial benefit. Evaluate these against the return threshold.
The Counter-Argument
The Tier 1 assets carry an average maintenance cost above $0.50–$0.77/mile — well above fleet average. Every month of deferral adds real maintenance spend on trucks that should already be replaced. Estimated additional maintenance exposure on deferred assets vs. prime-age benchmark: ~$474,376 over the deferral period (using 12–36 month cohort median of $0.2419/mi as the baseline). This cost doesn't appear in the ROI framework at all.
3
The 2028 Group Is Evaluated in Isolation
Low-return label ignores that this capital will be spent regardless — just at a higher price
Challenge
The Framework Says
The 2028 group returns only ~4.7% annually. Below hurdle rates. Do not proceed.
The Counter-Argument
Passing on the 2028 group doesn't eliminate the capital need — it defers it at full standard pricing with no negotiated discounts and no dealer leverage. Buying now vs. deferring saves $778,500 in purchase price alone. The "4.7% return" framing obscures the real question: why spend $778,500 more for the same trucks?
4
The Dealer Window Is a One-Time Event
Negotiated pricing, ground stock, and relationship leverage don't persist — they expire with this transaction
Challenge
The Framework Says
Evaluate each opportunity using a consistent return threshold going forward.
The Counter-Argument
The current pricing reflects: (1) a one-time contract discount on 20′ Slides, (2) negotiated reductions on Tractors and Flatbeds achieved through fleet volume leverage, and (3) a window before EPA emission upcharges apply. None of these conditions are guaranteed to recur. A 10% hurdle rate applied uniformly doesn't account for the time-limited nature of this opportunity.
5
The 10% Simple Rule Ignores Operational Risk
For a delivery fleet, uptime isn't just a financial metric — overaged assets carry real operational exposure
Challenge
The Framework Says
Only pre-purchase assets that generate ~10%+ annual return. Apply this as a simple, consistent rule.
The Counter-Argument
McCoy's operates a route delivery and jobsite fleet. A truck breakdown on a jobsite delivery isn't just a maintenance event — it's a customer service failure with downstream revenue and relationship risk. Four Tier 1 assets have already exceeded both age and mileage lifecycle simultaneously. The operational risk premium of running these assets beyond lifecycle doesn't appear in a financial return calculation but is very real.
Where We Agree
The framework makes valid points — these should be acknowledged, not dismissed
Valid Points in the Original Framework
✓Not all pre-buys are equal — the Slide discount is the strongest opportunity. The framework is right to differentiate.
✓Broad, undisciplined pre-purchasing is risky — buying trucks outside the replacement pipeline just because pricing is favorable doesn't hold up. This analysis is anchored to lifecycle data.
✓Each opportunity should be evaluated individually — agreed. This analysis does exactly that: tiered by lifecycle urgency, CPM, and body type with separate savings calculations per group.
✓Capital has a cost — acknowledged. The TCO tab accounts for this by showing the full cost of deferral rather than only the savings benefit.
The Right Question to Ask
The original framework asks: "Does this pre-buy generate a sufficient return on capital?"

The better question is: "What does it cost McCoy's to buy these same trucks in 2027–2028 instead of now?"

Since replacement is not optional, the decision is purely about price timing. The comparison below shows the fully-loaded cost of buying now vs. deferring — including purchase price, negotiated savings, emission avoidance, and disposition proceeds.
